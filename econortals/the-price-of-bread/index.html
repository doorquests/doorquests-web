              What this simulation assumes
              • Bread is one identical product. Every loaf is the same, and everyone knows the price.
              • Many small buyers and sellers: no single baker or household can set the market price alone.
              • On Market Day, each household wants exactly one loaf and each baker has one batch to sell.
              • In the town-wide model, demand and supply are straight lines. Events shift a whole curve; they do not change its slope.
              • In the Lean Year, households are grouped into 60 comfortable and 60 struggling households with different demand.
              • Producers respond to the price they received in Season 1 when planting and shipping for Season 2. The strength of this response (8 more loaves of capacity per penny above 11, capped) is an illustrative assumption.
              How the model works

              Market Day (Bronze). At a test price P, quantity demanded is the number of households whose maximum is at least P; quantity supplied is the number of bakers whose cost is at most P. Loaves sold = the smaller of the two. When loaves are short, they go to households in queue order (a fixed, seeded queue), not to those who value bread most. When loaves are in surplus, the lowest-cost bakers sell first. The clearing price is where wanted equals offered (11 pennies, 8 loaves). In the adjustment demonstration, sellers raise their asking price by 1 penny after a shortage and lower it by 1 after a surplus.

              Changing Seasons (Silver). Demand Qd = a − 20P, supply Qs = c + 20P, starting at a = 420, c = −20. Equilibrium: P* = (a − c) / 40, Q* = a − 20P*. A demand event changes a; a supply event changes c. The chart is drawn from these same equations.

              The Lean Year (Gold). Comfortable demand 240 − 10P; struggling demand 180 − 10(P − V), where V is the voucher value. Local supply max(0, c + 20P) with c = −140 in Season 1. Imports and granary releases add a fixed quantity. With no binding ceiling, the price solves demand = supply (by bisection). With a binding ceiling, the price is held at the ceiling, bakers offer only what they will at that price, and the gap is unmet demand. Shortage loaves go either by queue (shared in proportion to each group's demand at that price) or by equal ration per household. Season 2 local supply: c = −80 + incentive + 30 if roads are repaired, where incentive = 8 × (Season-1 price − 11), limited to between −8 and +40. Imports: half arrive in Season 1 (all with road repairs) unless a storm (seeded, 1 in 3) delays them; all arrive in Season 2. Producer earnings are the area between price and the local supply curve, shown as a percentage of a normal year. Readiness = granary stock left + Season-2 local capacity at 11 pennies.

              Where reality differs

              Real markets include bargaining, contracts, sellers with market power, different qualities of bread, transport costs, storage and hoarding, informal resale when prices are controlled, time spent in queues, changing expectations, and governments with limited ability to enforce rules. Real households are not two neat groups, and real producers respond with delays and uncertainty this model leaves out.

              What the evidence supports

              EstablishedIn a competitive market, a price held below the market-clearing level creates excess quantity demanded (a shortage); a price above it creates excess quantity supplied. Other things equal, a fall in supply raises price and lowers quantity; a rise in demand raises both.

              Well-supportedProducers respond more to prices over longer periods than in the short run. When supply is fixed in the short run, extra purchasing power (such as vouchers) can partly show up as higher prices. Research on famines, including work associated with Amartya Sen, finds that hunger often comes from people losing the ability to obtain food, not only from the total amount of food available.

              Contested · depends on contextWhich response works best in a real food crisis (letting prices rise, price controls, rationing, transfers, imports, reserves, or combinations) depends on enforcement, trade access, timing, institutions and how effects are weighed. Economists disagree about the size of these effects in particular cases.

              What the simulation cannot establish

              This is a simplified fictional model with numbers chosen for clarity. It cannot predict what any real policy would do in any real place. Its purpose is to show how the parts of a market interact under stated assumptions, so you can reason about real cases with better questions.
