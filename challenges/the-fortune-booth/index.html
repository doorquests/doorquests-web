      A note on carnivals, fortune booths and this puzzle

      The setting. Travelling carnivals grew out of the midways of late nineteenth-century fairs: Chicago's 1893 World's Columbian Exposition set the pattern with its Midway Plaisance of rides, sideshows, games of chance and food stands, and travelling carnival companies followed in its wake. One history counts 17 travelling carnivals in the United States in 1902 and more than 300 by 1936. Carnivals offered sideshows and games of skill and chance, and they soon had a reputation for less than perfect honesty with their customers.

      What is fiction. This booth, its curtains and its fortune-teller, Augusta Lark, are invented. Her “predictions” are stage patter: nobody in this game can see the future, and the game does not present fortune-telling as real. Augusta is an original character; she is not modelled on, and does not stand for, any real person, people or culture.

      The mathematics is modern. The switch-or-stay puzzle is known today as the Monty Hall problem, after the host of the 1960s American television game show Let's Make a Deal. It was posed by the biostatistician Steve Selvin in a 1975 letter to The American Statistician and became famous in 1990 through Marilyn vos Savant's “Ask Marilyn” column in Parade. Nobody claims it was played in a 1920s booth; the game simply sets it there. Like the classic statement of the puzzle, every rule here is spelled out, because the answer depends on exactly how the host chooses.

      Sources: Encyclopedia of Mathematics (European Mathematical Society), “Monty Hall problem”; Encyclopaedia Britannica, “carnival”; Faye Charpentier, “The Origins of the American Traveling Carnival” (Folkstreams); International Independent Showmen's Museum photograph collection, “Midway, Carnival in New York” (circa 1920), hosted by the University of South Florida Libraries. Full addresses are in the game's documentation.
