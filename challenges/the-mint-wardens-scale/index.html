    A note on the Great Recoinage, Isaac Newton, and this game

    The Great Recoinage. By the 1690s much of England's hammered silver coinage was clipped or worn, and some coins held less silver than their face value. On 13 January 1696 Parliament passed the Act for Remedying the Ill State of the Coin of the Kingdom: the old silver was to be called in and coined afresh to the old Elizabethan standard, and old hammered coin had to be handed in by July 1696. The new coins were struck by machine with screw presses and milled edges, which made them more uniform and harder to clip or counterfeit. Country mints opened at Bristol, Exeter, Norwich, York and Chester, and coined about a quarter of the £6.8 million in new silver. The recoinage ran until the end of 1699.

    Isaac Newton at the Mint. The Royal Mint was then housed in the Tower of London. Isaac Newton was appointed Warden of the Mint in the spring of 1696, on the recommendation of Charles Montague, Chancellor of the Exchequer, and arrived at the Tower that April. As Warden, his main duty was to investigate and bring to justice those who clipped and counterfeited the coin. He wanted coins made to the correct weight and fineness. He became Master of the Mint with effect from Christmas Day 1699, and held that office until his death in March 1727.

    What is fiction, and what is modern. The warden in this game is an invented character, not Newton, and he speaks no words of Newton's. His chamber, his commissions and his counterfeit coins are fiction, and the room is imagined, not a reconstruction of any documented room. The twelve-coin puzzle is a modern mathematical problem: it first appeared in print in 1945, in Howard D. Grossman's “The twelve-coin problem” in Scripta Mathematica. Nothing here suggests that Newton or the Mint ever used it. The sealed note, the rule that every weighing must be certain to finish, and the scoring are the game's own.
