    A note on Edo-period checkpoints, and what is fiction here

    The checkpoints. Under the Tokugawa shogunate (1603–1868), travel on the great highways passed through official checkpoints, called sekisho. The most famous, on the Tōkaidō road between Edo and Kyoto, was at Hakone, established on its site by Lake Ashi in 1619. The checkpoints were principally concerned with security: they controlled weapons coming toward Edo and women leaving it, since the families of the feudal lords were kept in Edo. Travelers needed approved permits to pass, the gates opened only in daylight, and trying to get around a checkpoint was a serious crime. Women officials examined female travelers. The Meiji government abolished the checkpoints in 1869.

    Hakone today. The Hakone checkpoint has been reconstructed on its original site. A detailed report of repairs from 1865, found in 1983, together with excavation, guided the rebuilding of its gates and guardhouses. Even so, the colours of the officials' clothing are not known; the reconstruction's figures are displayed in white. The scene in this game takes inspiration from such timber gates and tiled guardhouses, but it is not a reconstruction of Hakone or of any real checkpoint.

    What is fiction. The checkpoint, its two gates, the wardens, the head inspector, the traveler and the puzzle are all invented. Truth-telling and lying wardens were not an Edo-period custom; the riddle is a modern logic puzzle in a historical setting. The wardens' indigo, olive and umber coats are the game's own choice, used only to tell them apart. KA and TO are invented game tokens, not Japanese words; no Japanese phrases are put in anyone's mouth. Signboards in the scene are labelled in English for play.

    Sources: Hakone Tourist Association, “Sekisho: the Hakone Checkpoint”; Journey Through Edo's Legacy (Tokyo Metropolitan Government), on Hakone Sekisho; the reference article “Hakone Barrier” (citing Japan's Agency for Cultural Affairs). Full addresses are in the game's documentation.
