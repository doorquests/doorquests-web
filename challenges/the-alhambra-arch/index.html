        A note on the Nasrids and their tilework

        From 1232 until 1492 the Nasrid dynasty ruled the kingdom of Granada, the last Muslim state on the Iberian Peninsula. On a hill above the city they built the Alhambra, less a single palace than a royal city of courts, halls, gardens and watercourses, raised over nearly two centuries. Much of its most celebrated decoration dates from the fourteenth-century reigns of Yusuf I (1333–54) and Muhammad V (1354–59, 1362–91).

        Its rooms combine carved plaster, carved wood and glazed ceramics. Geometric pattern is one of the great families of ornament in Islamic art, alongside calligraphy and vegetal design: patterns grown from simple forms such as squares, circles and stars, repeated, interlaced and balanced so that they could in principle continue without end.

        In alicatado, small glazed tiles were cut or moulded into shapes and fitted together like a mosaic. Surviving dado panels at the Alhambra use blue, ochre, green and black on a white ground, in interlaced bands and eight-pointed stars; repetition, careful proportion and the alternation of colours give the walls their rhythm. This game's glazes (cobalt, turquoise, green, honey, ivory and manganese black) are inspired by that palette.

        What is modern here: the workshop, the master and apprentice, the three commissions and the "drawing" are fiction. The triangular lattice, the exact-fit rules, the shapes of the tiles and the colour marks are a modern educational puzzle, not a reconstruction of how Nasrid craftsmen were trained or how their designs were laid out. No inscription or religious symbol is reproduced.

        Sources: The Metropolitan Museum of Art, The Art of the Nasrid Period (1232–1492) and Geometric Patterns in Islamic Art (Heilbrunn Timeline of Art History); Patronato de la Alhambra y Generalife, The tile panel from the Mexuar Room (Piece of the Month).
