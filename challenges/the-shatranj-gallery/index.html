    A historical note: shatranj and the old elephant

    Shatranj was the form of chess played across the Persian and Arab world in the early Islamic centuries, inherited from India's chaturanga by way of Sasanian Persia. Caliphs and scholars studied it, and masters wrote collections of its problems.

    The horse (faras) leapt as the knight still does: two squares one way and one across, over anything in between.

    The elephant (al-fil, from Persian pil) leapt exactly two squares diagonally, over the square between. It could never take a single diagonal step, and on the whole board each elephant could reach only eight squares. The modern bishop's long diagonal move is a much later European rule (late fifteenth century) and is not the al-fil's move.

    The palace, its gallery and its ceremony are fiction, inspired by the architecture and the game of the period. This is a pathfinding puzzle, not a game of chess: there is no opponent and no checkmate.
