      A note on Jacquard and the punched card

      Before punched cards, a figured silk loom needed a second worker, the drawboy, to pull the cords that lifted the right warp threads for each throw of the shuttle. Lyon's weavers and mechanics spent the eighteenth century trying to automate that choice: Basile Bouchon used a perforated paper band in 1725, Jean-Baptiste Falcon a chain of punched cards a few years later, and Jacques de Vaucanson an automatic mechanism around 1740.

      Joseph Marie Jacquard, a Lyon man, studied Vaucanson's loom in Paris in 1804 and refined the idea into the mechanism that bears his name. Napoleon granted it to the city of Lyon in 1805, though many weavers feared it, and it spread widely only after further improvements in the following decade. In a Jacquard machine each card corresponds to one row of the design, one throw of the shuttle. Where the card has a hole, a needle passes through and its hook stays in place to be lifted; where there is no hole, the hook is pushed aside. Each hook raises not a single thread but a group of warp threads through the harness cords, and that choice, row by row, is what makes the figure appear in the cloth.

      The Rivoire workshop, its master weaver, its apprentice, its loom and its three commissions are fiction, an experimental cousin of the real machines. The grid in this game is a simplified, playable abstraction: one position stands for one hook and its group of threads, and two-colour rows imagine one card for each coloured shuttle. A real card was never simply a picture made of holes.

      The puzzle form itself, the nonogram or picture-logic puzzle, is a twentieth-century invention.
