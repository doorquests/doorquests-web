      A note on the Pythagoreans, their pebbles, and what is fiction here

      Pythagoras and his followers. Pythagoras of Samos (about 570–490 BCE) moved to Croton, a Greek city on the coast of southern Italy (Magna Graecia), and the community associated with him spread among the Greek cities there. Little about him is certain: Pythagoras wrote nothing, and the fullest accounts of his life were written some eight hundred years after his death, full of legend. Even the theorem that bears his name is not attributed to him in the early sources. Our clearest evidence for early Pythagorean ideas comes from fifth-century figures such as Philolaus, and from what Aristotle later reported about “the Pythagoreans”.

      Number and form with pebbles. Greeks counted and calculated with small stones, psephoi. Aristotle and his pupil Theophrastus report that Eurytus, a Pythagorean of about 400 BCE, arranged pebbles to show the number that defined a thing. Judging from Aristotle’s account, the Pythagoreans also built figures of dots or pebbles: odd numbers set around a single unit as right-angled “gnomons” (carpenter’s squares) always make squares, while even gnomons make oblong figures; triangular numbers (3, 6, 10, 15, 21 …) grow by adding a row one longer than the last. The pair odd and even, and the pair square and oblong, appear in the table of opposites that Aristotle attributes to some Pythagoreans. The tetractys, a triangle of ten points in rows of one, two, three and four, became a revered Pythagorean symbol, though scholars doubt that it goes back to Pythagoras himself. Later handbooks, such as that of Nicomachus of Gerasa (about AD 100), set out this arithmetic of figures systematically.

      What is fiction. The school by the sea, the Rule Keeper, the apprentice, the trials and the Keeper’s YES and NO are all invented. No source says the Pythagoreans played a game like this one, and the game’s hidden laws are modern puzzles. The board is a square grid, so the game draws triangular numbers as right-angled staircases (rows of 1, 2, 3 … flush to one side) rather than the equal-sided triangles of Greek diagrams; the count is the same.

      Sources: Carl Huffman, “Pythagoras” and “Pythagoreanism”, Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (revised 2024); Encyclopaedia Britannica, “Pythagoreanism: Metaphysics and number theory” and “gnomon”; the reference articles “Figurate number” and “Nicomachus”.
