      A note on Knossos, the myth of the Labyrinth, and what is fiction here

      Archaeology. Knossos, near Heraklion in Crete, is the largest Bronze Age palace site of the Minoan civilisation. Its great palace complex, with courtyards, light wells, storerooms and painted plaster walls, flourished in the second millennium BC. It was excavated from 1900 by Arthur Evans, whose concrete reconstructions (including the red columns that taper downward) still shape how visitors see the site; the British School at Athens continues research there. Bulls, bull-leaping scenes and the double axe (labrys) appear in Minoan art and cult.

      Myth. Much later Greek stories tell of King Minos, the Minotaur kept in a Labyrinth built by Daedalus, Theseus who killed it, and Ariadne, whose thread led him out. Some have linked the word “labyrinth” with the labrys and the sprawling palace itself, but that connection is debated, and no Labyrinth of the myth has been found. Ariadne's thread is a mythological motif.

      What is fiction. The rotating ring chamber, its mechanism, the Keeper, the explorer, the three tokens (the Bronze Bull, the Double Axe and the Spiral Seal) and the three commissions are invented for this game. No mechanically turning labyrinth beneath Knossos is known, and none is claimed; the tokens are gameplay objects, not artefacts.

      Sources: Ashmolean Museum, “Myths of the Labyrinth”; British School at Athens, “Knossos Research Centre”; Hellenic Heritage, “Knossos”. Full addresses are in the game's documentation.
