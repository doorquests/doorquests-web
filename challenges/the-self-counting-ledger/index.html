    A note on Pacioli, Venetian bookkeeping, and what is fiction here

    Venice, 1494. Luca Pacioli's Summa de arithmetica, geometria, proportioni et proportionalita was printed in Venice in 1494 by Paganino Paganini. Among its many parts is a treatise on bookkeeping, Particularis de computis et scripturis, one of the first printed descriptions of the double-entry method used by Venetian and other northern Italian merchants: every transaction entered twice, debits against credits, so that the books can be checked and balanced. Pacioli did not invent double entry; merchants had used it for about two centuries, and he recorded and explained their practice.

    Self-describing puzzles are modern. Numbers that count their own digits, such as the ones in this ledger, are a recreational topic of modern mathematics. Sentences that inventory their own letters were named “autograms” by Lee Sallows; Douglas Hofstadter published the first of them in his Scientific American column in 1982. None of this is Pacioli's, and these are not exercises from 1494.

    What is fiction. The counting room, the mathematician, the apprentice and the three examinations are invented for this game. “Balancing” here is an analogy: the brass scale weighs declared totals against counted ones, which is not a Renaissance accounting technique.
