    A note on medieval time-reckoning, and what is fiction here

    The computus. Medieval scholars called the art of reckoning the calendar, and above all the date of Easter, the computus. In the Latin West, Easter was reckoned by the method of Dionysius Exiguus (sixth century), which Bede of Jarrow explained and championed in his treatise On the Reckoning of Time (725). Bede's work helped establish dating by the year of the Lord (AD) across Europe.

    Three cycles, not one. The reckoning rests on a nineteen-year lunar cycle: the ecclesiastical paschal full moons fall on the same dates every nineteen years. The weekdays keep a different rhythm. In the Julian calendar the pattern of weekdays across the year repeats only every 28 years (the solar cycle), because a leap day falls every fourth year. Easter needs both the moon and the Sunday, so its dates do not simply repeat after nineteen years: the same Easter dates return in the same order only after 19 × 28 = 532 years, the great paschal cycle. That is why a medieval Easter table had to list the years one by one.

    The method in this game. The calendar is the Julian calendar, the one in use in western Europe around 1200: every fourth year is a leap year, the century years included. The year's place in the nineteen-year cycle is its Golden Number, the remainder of the year divided by 19, plus 1. Each Golden Number fixes the date of the paschal full moon (luna XIV, the fourteenth day of the ecclesiastical moon), read from a fixed table; this is a reckoned moon, not the astronomical one. Easter is the first Sunday after that full moon, so between 22 March and 25 April. The concurrent, the weekday of 24 March counted from 1 for Sunday to 7 for Saturday, was one of the medieval tools for finding weekdays. Church Latin numbered the weekdays as feriae: Monday feria secunda through Friday feria sexta, with Saturday sabbatum and Sunday dominica. Every figure in the game's table was computed by these rules and checked independently, by two methods; Easter 1215, for instance, fell on 19 April, as the Magna Carta Project's dating of King John's letters records.

    Real manuscripts. Such material survives in many medieval books. The British Library's Royal MS 12 D IV, copied at Canterbury in the first half of the twelfth century, contains a calendar with computus tables (among them an Easter table for 1090–1402), Helperic's treatise on the computus, and Bede's On the Reckoning of Time. A twelfth-century Durham manuscript in the University of Glasgow Library (MS Hunter 85) gathers a calendar, Bede's nineteen-year cycles and his treatise.

    What is fiction. The monastery, its librarian, its senior computist, the apprentice and the speaking doors are all invented. No monastery kept doors that lied on certain weekdays; the truth-and-lie schedules are a modern logic puzzle set in a historical place. The manuscript in this game is an original construction, not a copy of any real table, though its figures follow the medieval rules exactly. Years here are numbered from 1 January; medieval writers sometimes began the year at Christmas or on 25 March. The Gold commission is set on 21 March, the day on which St Benedict's principal feast was traditionally kept (modern calendars keep it on 11 July).
