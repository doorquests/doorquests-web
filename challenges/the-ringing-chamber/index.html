    How to play, controls and scoring
      A note on change ringing

      English change ringing grew up in church towers from the seventeenth century onward. A band rings a set of tuned bells in sequence, and each ringer controls one bell with its rope. Because a heavy bell swinging full circle can only alter its timing a little, a bell moves at most one place from one row to the next. So the order changes by neighbouring bells swapping places.

      In called changes the order changes only when the conductor calls. Different towers use different conventions; this game uses "calling up", where "2 to 3" means bell 2 moves up to ring after bell 3. In method ringing the ringers follow a learned pattern, such as plain hunt or Plain Bob, and the order changes at every row. Ringing every possible row once is an extent: on four bells (minimus) that is 4! = 24 rows. A formal peal is far longer, at least 5,000 changes, so the 24-row extent here is a complete extent, not a peal.

      St Mary's, Elderstowe, its captain and its ceremony are fiction. The terms, the plain hunt, the calling-up convention, the named rows Queens and Tittums, and Plain Bob Minimus are real.
