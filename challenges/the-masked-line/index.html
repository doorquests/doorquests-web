    A note on the Carnival of Venice, and what is fiction here

    The carnival. Masks were part of Venetian life for centuries. The earliest surviving laws that mention them date from the thirteenth century, when the Great Council forbade masked revelers from throwing scented eggs; a law of 1339 forbade vulgar disguises and visiting convents while masked, and the mask-makers had their own guild statute by 1436. The carnival was most famous in the eighteenth century, when masks were permitted for months each winter and the bauta, a full-face mask worn with a tricorn hat and black cloak, had become a standard disguise. The festival was outlawed in 1797 under Emperor Francis II after the fall of the Republic, returned only briefly in the nineteenth century, and was revived in 1979.

    The masks in the scenes. The revelers wear simple half-masks in three colours with distinct patterns, chosen for the puzzle's legibility; they are not copies of historical masks. Several masks familiar today, such as the half-mask called the Colombina, are modern creations.

    What is fiction. The master, the company and the examination are invented for this game. The line puzzle itself is a modern recreational problem, usually told with prisoners and hats; it is not a Venetian invention and was not played at the carnival.

    Sources: the reference articles “Carnival of Venice” and “Hat puzzle”. Full addresses are in the game's documentation.
