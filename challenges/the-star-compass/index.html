      A note on Pacific wayfinding, the star compass, and this game

      Traditional Pacific wayfinding. The peoples of the Pacific settled thousands of islands across vast distances, and many navigation traditions grew among different peoples, islands and languages; they were not one identical system. Navigators read the rising and setting of stars, the Sun, ocean swells, winds, birds and clouds, and kept track of the canoe's movement, knowledge passed on through long training and practice at sea. In Hawaiʻi the voyaging revival began in the 1970s with the canoe Hōkūleʻa: in 1976 Mau Piailug, a master navigator from Satawal in Micronesia, guided her to Tahiti without instruments, and in 1980 Nainoa Thompson, who trained with him, navigated her from Tahiti back to Hawaiʻi.

      The modern Hawaiian star compass. The star compass in this game follows the modern Hawaiian star compass taught by the Polynesian Voyaging Society, which credits its Hawaiian form to Mau Piailug and Nainoa Thompson's teaching. It is a mental framework, not a physical instrument: the horizon is divided into 32 houses, each 11.25° wide, with the cardinal points Hikina (east), Hema (south), Komohana (west) and ʻĀkau (north), the quadrants Koʻolau, Malanai, Kona and Hoʻolua, and the house names Lā, ʻĀina, Noio, Manu, Nālani, Nāleo and Haka repeated in each quadrant. A star rising in one house sets in the house of the same name on the opposite side. This modern framework should not be taken as the unchanged system of every ancient navigator. The Hawaiian star names shown (such as Hōkūpaʻa for Polaris and Puana-kau for Rigel) are as listed by the Polynesian Voyaging Society.

      What this game simplifies. The voyage, its people and its islands are fiction. The shadow plate, the rising-point diagram and the chart are modern teaching aids, and Gold's arithmetic of vectors (velocity over ground equals velocity through the water plus the current) is a contemporary abstraction on a small flat chart, not how traditional navigators worked, and not adequate for long ocean distances. Three puzzles are an introduction to a few principles, not a substitute for real instruction in wayfinding. Rising directions are computed for the stars' catalogue positions at 20° N without refraction; the noon Sun is computed for 21° N at the December solstice. At other latitudes and seasons the noon shadow can point south, or nearly vanish.
