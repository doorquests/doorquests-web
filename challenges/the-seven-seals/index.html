      A note on Byzantine seals and scriptoria, and what is fiction here

      Seals. In Byzantium, seals were used to authenticate and to secure letters and documents. The great majority that survive are of lead: a lead blank with a channel through it was threaded on the string that closed the document and struck in a press, so that the document could not be opened without breaking it. Emperors used gold, and a few rulers silver; wax was also used. Seals usually carry an image (the Virgin, a saint, a cross, sometimes an animal) and an inscription naming the owner with his rank and office, which is why collections such as the thousands of seals at Dumbarton Oaks are a major source for Byzantine administration.

      The offices. The owners in the register are real Byzantine titles: a logothete (a senior administrator), the eparch (the city prefect of Constantinople), the chartophylax (the patriarchate's keeper of records), a hegoumenos (the head of a monastery), a strategos (a military governor), a protospatharios (a high court dignity), and a notary. Manuscripts were copied in monastic and imperial workshops; the scriptorium in the scenes is imaginary.

      What is fiction. The archive, the archivist, the courier, the coloured cords, the destinations' pairing with particular owners and emblems, and the three puzzles are invented for this game. The combinations are not claimed to be historical, and this kind of deduction puzzle is not documented in Byzantine history.

      Sources: Dumbarton Oaks, “Byzantine Seals: Introduction”; the reference articles “Byzantine bureaucracy and aristocracy”, “Chartophylax”, “Hegumen” and “Scriptorium”. Full addresses are in the game's documentation.
