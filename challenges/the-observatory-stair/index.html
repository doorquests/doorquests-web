      A note on El Caracol and Maya numbers

      Established history. Chichén Itzá, in Yucatán, Mexico, flourished from about 600 to 1200 CE. Among its buildings is El Caracol, “the snail”, a round building set on two rectangular platforms of different orientation and named for the spiral staircase inside it. Its present form is the result of at least six phases of remodelling. Mexico's Instituto Nacional de Antropología e Historia describes it as a place that may have served for astronomical observation; the details of its alignments are debated, and this game claims none.

      Maya numbers were written with three signs: a dot for one, a bar (often drawn as a stick) for five, and a shell for zero. Numbers were counted in twenties and written vertically, with the largest place at the top. Scholars still discuss whether the shell was thought of as a true zero or as a placeholder. In the calendrical Long Count the third place was changed to 18 × 20 = 360, close to a year; this game does not use that calendar count. It uses pure base 20 throughout: units at the bottom, then twenties, then four-hundreds.

      Living Maya. Maya peoples are not only history. Millions of Maya people live today in Mexico and Central America, speaking many Maya languages; contemporary Maya farmers still practise observation-based astronomy, and the 260-day calendar is kept in daily use in the highlands of Guatemala.

      Artistic reconstruction. The tower, stair, chamber, attendant and carved panels in this game are original artwork inspired by El Caracol; they do not reproduce the building's real plan.

      Modern puzzle mechanics. The sequences, the woven paths and the self-describing inscription are modern mathematical puzzles. They are not records found at El Caracol, and nothing suggests Maya observers used look-and-say sequences; the game uses Maya numerals only to write its own puzzles.

      Sources: Instituto Nacional de Antropología e Historia, Chichén Itzá (lugares.inah.gob.mx); Smithsonian National Museum of the American Indian, Living Maya Time: “The Calendar System” and the Maya Math Game; Mathematical Association of America, Convergence, on Maya numerals. Full addresses are in the game's documentation.
