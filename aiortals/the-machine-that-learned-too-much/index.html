The Assay Hall at Glasswick

For decades a brass-and-glass machine in this salt-coast hall sorted three kinds of lumenstone. ▲ Ember stones are warm, dense and many-ringed. ● Tide stones are cool and dim. ■ Hollow stones are light, bright and rare. Its last keeper, the archivist Wren Halloway, left a logbook. The final entry reads: It never made a mistake on the old stones. Then the new quarry opened.

You are the new keeper. The Sorting Engine is a real, simplified machine-learning system running in this page: k-nearest neighbours, decision trees and logistic regression, trained on fictional, seeded data. Every score you see is computed from the stones in front of you.

Your first minute: the first tray

Here are 24 labelled stones. Choose two measurements and teach the engine. It starts by consulting a single neighbour: each stone is sorted like the most similar stone it has seen.

This control is specific to k-nearest neighbours: a small k draws intricate borders around every stone; a large k draws smoother ones. Trees and logistic regression have their own, different complexity settings.

Wren’s logbook flutters open: A perfect score on stones it has already seen tells you nothing about stones it hasn’t.

How investigations work
  • There are 20 investigations in four acts. Each has three separate tasks: Discover (gentle), Investigate (medium) and Verify (rigorous).
  • Use the lab tools to run real experiments, then record your findings and submit them to the judge. The judge recomputes everything from the data and your configuration. Clicking a tool never completes anything by itself.
  • Some checks use a large hidden assessment shipment. It is hidden from the interface, not from the page source: this is a teaching tool, not an exam system.
  • Each task has 12 shipments (fresh seeded draws). Every shipment has been checked solvable by a reference solution. Changing shipment starts the task on new stones.
  • Every control works with a keyboard. Every chart has a table and a text summary.
