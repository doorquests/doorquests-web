> Tuning an old piano allows a technician to hear the voices of musicians from decades ago.

## Premise

The **Hallam Conservatory** closes on Friday after **111 years**. Its contents are due to be auctioned on Saturday, including a **1911 Bechstein upright** in **Room 4B**. The **Crane Foundation** wants the instrument for its museum, where it will be displayed as *the piano on which Edmund Crane composed the Crane Nocturne*.

You are the piano technician assigned to service it before the auction. The A above middle C is flat. As its sound fades, you hear something almost like a human voice.

The instrument seems to **remember the frequencies to which it has been tuned**. At four particular A4 pitches, music and voices from different decades become audible. Their recollections reveal who actually composed the celebrated work—and whose names were removed from its history.

The narrative uses sound not as decoration but as a way to discover evidence, hear testimony and finally restore authorship.

## How to play

Each narrative scene offers three numbered choices: click a choice or press **1**, **2**, or **3**. **Retrace your last step** undoes the latest decision; **Start over** returns to the first note.

The wooden tuning bench contains a switch marked **♪ Sound**. Audio starts **off** and is activated by the player. Once enabled, tuning and the two-octave keyboard produce synthesized, piano-like sounds; historical musical phrases and the reconstructed score can also be played. The audio is **generated through browser synthesis with layered harmonics**, not sampled recordings of an acoustic piano. Reading and navigating remain possible without sound.

Previously reached endings are remembered in browser-local storage. The reviewed website version also saves the current scene and history.

## The Tuning Bench

A walnut-and-ivory interface above the story contains:

- **A4 frequency readout:** Current pitch in hertz, plus deviation in **cents** from 440 Hz concert pitch.
- **Tuning gauge:** A moving needle with marked settings at **432, 435, 440, 444 and 452 Hz**.
- **String-strain indicator:** Six strain pips warn that repeated changes can snap the century-old A string.
- **Playable keyboard:** **C4–B5**, with individual keys aligned to the current tuning and recovered phrase tones highlighted.
- **Sound toggle:** Enables or silences audio only after user interaction.

The instrument begins at **427 Hz**. A change to a different ordinary target pitch adds **one** strain point; a change to **452 Hz** adds **three**. Strain reaching **six** during a continuing narrative transition triggers **The Broken String**. Visiting a frequency already tuned to does not create an additional frequency-change penalty. The extreme 452-Hz scene also offers an explicit choice that snaps the string.

Because the strongest-ending route can hear the four voices in **four changes**, avoid unnecessary retuning and do not attempt the risky 452-Hz pull on that route.

## Pitch as historical testimony

| A4 tuning | Historical year | Voice | Musical or archival clue |
|---|---:|---|---|
| **435 Hz** | **1938** | **Hanna Weiss** | Viennese teacher; her tuning fork is stamped `a¹ = 435 · WIEN`. Establishes the four-bar tradition and shared authorship rule. |
| **440 Hz** | **1958** | **Ruth Adair** | Young pianist; her ascending phrase and subsequent conversation reveal Director Crane's appropriation of the work. |
| **444 Hz** | **1971** | **Desmond Hale** | Jazz musician; his blue note leads to the account of a manuscript hidden under the treble bottom board. |
| **432 Hz** | **1994** | **Priya Mehta** | Ten-year-old pupil; her rising arpeggio is connected to the present-day Dean, **Dr. Priya Mehta**. |
| **452 Hz** | — | **All voices overlapping** | Excessive tension produces tangled voices and puts the old wire at serious risk. |

The work initially credited to **Edmund Crane** is in fact a chain of contributions spanning multiple generations.

## The mystery and the hidden manuscript — spoilers

**1938 — Hanna Weiss:** A Viennese custom follows her to Hallam. Each student learning in Room 4B contributes four bars to a piece that is never considered finished: *the rent*. Her overriding rule is that **no single person may claim the piece exclusively**.

**1958 — Ruth Adair:** Ruth contributes the next phrase. Director **Edmund Crane** takes possession of the composition in connection with the Hallam Prize, insists on his own prominent name, and threatens Ruth's career if she objects. In the piano's fading resonance she asks someone to preserve the truth.

**1971 — Desmond Hale:** Desmond plays a jazz-inflected phrase and knows that Crane intends to destroy the authentic score. He hides the manuscript **beneath the bottom board, under the treble strings**, behind modern brass screws, where a future technician may discover it.

**1994 — Priya Mehta:** Young Priya adds an ascending arpeggio, with Desmond's encouragement. Her initials appear on the hammer rail; she eventually becomes Hallam's Dean.

**2026 — The technician:** Inside the case is *Nocturne for Room 4B*, showing the successive contributors' names and blank staves marked **“for the next one.”** The technician can bring that score to the conservatory's farewell recital and either preserve the false attribution, disclose a partial truth, or complete the next musical contribution.

The manuscript is accessible through the tuning hub after the story has revealed enough historical evidence. The final truth is not simply that Crane was wrong: **the composition's living, collaborative tradition is worth continuing**.

## The Score, Voices and Pencil Marks

**The Score** is a compact musical staff in the sidebar. Recovered four-note phrases appear in colors assigned to each era; the fifth, initially blank phrase is reserved for the player. The **▶ Play the recovered score** control synthesizes the phrases already found, at their respective historical pitches.

**Voices in the Wood** tracks which of the four musical witnesses have been heard, showing the relevant year, person, and A4 setting.

**Pencil Marks** collects persistent investigative discoveries: the bench's Viennese fork and frequency notes, initials on the hammer rail, the shared-authorship rule, Crane's theft, Desmond's concealment, Priya's present identity, and finally the authentic manuscript.

## Six canonical endings

| ID | Ending | Condition and consequence |
|---|---|---|
| **E1** | **The Fifth Phrase** · *truest* | Hear **all four historical voices**, locate the manuscript, perform the recovered work at the farewell recital, and compose **four bars of your own**. The contributors' names are restored. |
| **E2** | **Concert Pitch** | Leave after standard tuning or give only polite remarks; the Bechstein enters the Crane Foundation's museum, with the false credit intact. |
| **E3** | **The Crane Nocturne** | Find the manuscript but sell it to **Oliver Crane**; evidence is destroyed and the museum's story remains unchanged. |
| **E4** | **A Rumour of Voices** | Report the voices without presenting the proof; another technician replaces the player and the auction proceeds. |
| **E5** | **The Broken String** | Overstrain or deliberately snap the piano's old A string, losing the resonance that carried its memories. |
| **E6** | **An Incomplete Score** | Locate the manuscript and attempt a public correction before hearing **all four** historical voices; the account remains vulnerable and the piano is sold. |

The HTML awards the strongest ending when the **manuscript is found** and **all four voice scenes have been visited**, then the player selects the full recital and one of **three original fifth-phrase variants**. The ending's authorship restoration is shared across all three choices.

## Walkthrough to the truest ending — spoilers

The uploaded guide provides this safe example route:

1. **Optional:** Open the piano bench and front panel. Examine the Vienna fork, frequency clues and four sets of initials.
2. Set **A = 440 Hz**. Hear **Ruth Adair (1958)**, and **keep listening** to learn about Crane's appropriation.
3. Set **A = 435 Hz**. Hear **Hanna Weiss (1938)**, and keep listening to learn the rule about shared authorship.
4. Set **A = 444 Hz**. Hear **Desmond Hale (1971)**, and keep listening for the manuscript's hiding place.
5. Set **A = 432 Hz**. Hear **Priya Mehta (1994)**, and keep listening to identify her as the current Dean.
6. Find the **brass-screwed bottom board beneath the treble** and remove it to retrieve *Nocturne for Room 4B*.
7. Proceed to the farewell recital and choose **“Play the nocturne as it was written: every phrase, every name.”**
8. When the piece reaches the empty staves, choose any of the **three four-bar phrases** and complete the fifth contribution.

This route requires **four ordinary pitch changes**, keeping string strain below the failure threshold. **Avoid 452 Hz**: a three-point increase on top of the preceding four changes would break the string.

The final moral principle is Hanna Weiss's: **“No one may put only one name on it.”**

## Characters

- **The technician:** Playable professional, responsible for tuning the Bechstein before auction and choosing whether to restore its history.
- **Hanna Weiss:** Viennese émigré teacher, first contributor in 1938.
- **Ruth Adair:** Young Hallam student whose contribution and credit were appropriated in 1958.
- **Edmund Crane:** Conservatory Director who took the manuscript and claimed the work.
- **Desmond Hale:** 1971 jazz contributor and later teacher who safeguarded the original manuscript.
- **Priya Mehta:** Ten-year-old student in 1994, now the Conservatory's Dean.

## Design, sound and accessibility

The visual identity resembles a historic piano workshop: dark walnut, ivory and ebony keys, brass fittings, tuning-gauge needle, and individually colored musical eras. Typography uses **Bodoni Moda** for the title, **Source Serif 4** for the story text and **IBM Plex Mono** for markings, measurement readouts and score metadata.

Sound uses the browser's **Web Audio API** with layered oscillators, harmonic partials and a decaying gain envelope. Sound begins off and is only enabled by a player action. A keyboard and score playback are playable through the interface; text choices also remain navigable without audio.

The presentation supports light/dark palettes, a responsive single-column layout below 900px, reduced-motion preferences, keyboard-selectable choices and piano keys, and collapsible reference sections in the reviewed mobile implementation.
