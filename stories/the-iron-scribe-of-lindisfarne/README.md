# The Iron Scribe of Lindisfarne

**The Axiom Continuum · Daily Door #29**  
**Genre:** Historical mystery / medieval fantasy / interactive moral drama  
**Setting:** The Holy Island of Lindisfarne, Northumbria · Anno Domini 793

**Documentation status:** Committed; Founder review pending

> A monastery discovers a manuscript describing events that have not occurred.

## Premise

On **20 March 793**, the Feast of Saint Cuthbert, the floor of the church of Saint Peter cracks beneath a procession of monks. Under a fallen stone slab lies an iron-bound manuscript: two black plates riveted together, fine calfskin pages and writing in iron-gall ink. Some lines are already dark; others are faint, as though they have not fully dried.

You are the monastery's **armarius**, keeper of its library. The book is dated to the current year, but describes events that **have yet to occur**, culminating in a June attack by raiders arriving from the sea. It predicts injuries, deaths, captivity and the destruction of the monastery's illuminated Gospel Book. Its final sentence stops midway: **“And the one who reads this shall…”**

The story asks whether foreknowledge makes a person responsible for preventing harm, and whether changing the future requires courage, evidence and restraint.

## How to play

At each scene choose between three options, numbered **i**, **ii** and **iii**. Click or tap them, or press keyboard **1**, **2** or **3**.

**Retrace your last step** undoes one decision; **Start over** returns to the discovery beneath the slab. Discovered endings are remembered in the browser. The reviewed standalone HTML additionally saves in-progress play and undo history locally.

Look at the **Liber Ferreus**, displayed at the top of the page. Its language and ink react to your decisions. What was once written as inevitable may be crossed out and replaced.

## Signature systems

### The Iron Book — *Liber Ferreus*

The manuscript is presented as an iron-bound, riveted book opened to a vellum page. It displays eight prophetic lines:

- **Approaching events:** Faint ink darkens as their dates come near.
- **Fulfilled prophecies:** The writing turns dark and is marked **fulfilled**.
- **Changed events:** Earlier lines are struck through, and altered outcomes appear in fresh brown ink marked **rewritten**.
- **The unfinished last line:** Its still-wet mark remains unresolved until the final decision.

The changing text is a central game-state display, rather than a static decorative illustration.

### Three reference panels

**The Brethren** tracks the developing fates of Brothers Wilfrith, Aldred, Cyneheard, Hunred, Abbot Higbald and the boy Ecgric.

**The Treasures** reports the condition of the illuminated Gospel Book, **St Cuthbert's coffin** and the iron-bound manuscript itself.

**The Witness** records the player's own consequential decisions: whom they informed, whether they warned or tested, whether the beacon was lit, and whom they helped escape.

## Story chronology and choices

### March: discovering what the book knows

On the feast of Saint Cuthbert, the armarius can read the manuscript alone, show it to **Brother Aldred**, or take it directly to **Abbot Higbald**. Higbald distrusts the artifact and wants it put away. Aldred calmly confronts the line foretelling his own death.

On **24 March**, strange lights and stormlike portents appear over the sea, fulfilling the manuscript's first line. The next prophecy concerns **Wilfrith**, a mason on the scaffolding of the new church.

### Easter: the cost of proof

The protagonist can:

1. **Warn Wilfrith**, keeping him off the scaffolding. The scaffold gives way with nobody on it, and the manuscript **rewrites itself** to reflect the changed future.
2. **Deliberately withhold the warning** to test the book. Wilfrith falls and breaks his leg.
3. **Dismiss the prediction** and fail to intervene, likewise allowing him to fall.

The first route demonstrates that fate can be altered. The second confirms the book's predictive power **at another person's expense**. Both routes can supply evidence at the abbot's council, but the injury carries a moral and mechanical consequence later.

### May: the Chapter House and the striped sail

At **Rogationtide**, **Brother Hunred**, the ambitious cellarer, proposes handing the iron book to **King Æthelred** as an instrument of power. The armarius can agree, argue for monastery preparations using evidence, or let the book remain hidden.

When persuaded with proof, Higbald concludes that the prophecy may be a warning rather than an unalterable decree, and grants permission to prepare for June.

At **Pentecost**, a ship with a striped red-and-white sail appears near the Farnes. The player may light the beacon on the Heugh, ride for the king's help at Bamburgh, or simply watch the vessel depart. The beacon choice changes the book's third line.

### June: evacuation and the final sentence

On **7 June**, the Pilgrims' Way is temporarily passable across the tidal sands. If the abbot has been convinced, the protagonist can bring **all fifty-one souls**, the Gospels and St Cuthbert's coffin to safety ahead of the raid.

Without his full cooperation, only some may leave, or the book may be hidden while people remain. On **8 June**, the raiders arrive. Afterward the armarius opens the iron book one final time: the unfinished sentence remains, and a pen lies ready.

The ultimate choice is between **burying the book with that sentence unfinished**, **writing the next line**, or **bringing the book to the king**.

## Characters

- **The armarius (you):** Keeper of the monastery's library, entrusted with the mysterious manuscript and its moral burden.
- **Abbot Higbald:** A cautious spiritual leader reluctant to follow a book without proof.
- **Brother Aldred:** Sixty-year-old illuminator, who has worked on the Gospel Book for forty years; the manuscript foretells his death at the altar.
- **Brother Wilfrith:** Mason whose predicted scaffold accident becomes the first test of whether written fate can be changed.
- **Brother Hunred:** Cellarer eager to trade foreknowledge for the king's patronage.
- **Brother Cyneheard and Ecgric:** A brother and a boy whose capture the manuscript foretells.
- **King Æthelred of Northumbria:** The ruler at Bamburgh to whom the book may be sent.

## Six canonical endings

| ID | Ending | How it occurs |
|---|---|---|
| **E1** | **The Unwritten Page** · *truest* | **Do not deliberately test Wilfrith**, convince Higbald through proof, **evacuate everyone**, then close and bury the iron book without finishing the last line |
| **E2** | **As It Was Written** | Allow the raid and the predicted tragedies to unfold without changing their outcomes, then close the book |
| **E3** | **The King's Book** | Send the prophetic book to King Æthelred, either at the council or after the raid; its power is used for political advantage |
| **E4** | **Half the Saints** | Achieve a **partial rescue**, save the Gospels alone, or evacuate all after having deliberately sacrificed Wilfrith to test the book |
| **E5** | **Heresy** | Insist on the dire June prophecy at the council **without proof**, leading to exile before the raid |
| **E6** | **The Iron Scribe** | Personally complete the book's last sentence, entering a cycle of writing events that then come true |

### Exact strongest-ending condition

The canonical playable code resolves the closing-the-book choice as follows:

```js
if (state.f.evacAll && !state.f.testW) return "E1";
if (state.f.evacAll || state.f.evacSome || state.f.hidden) return "E4";
return "E2";
```

**Important distinction:** The code excludes the strongest ending when `testW` was chosen—**deliberately** allowing Wilfrith to fall to test the prophecy. Simply dismissing the warning (`fell`) is a separate choice and is **not excluded by this exact conditional**, although the story's intended moral route is to warn him. A full evacuation can only be selected when Higbald has been convinced.

## Walkthrough to the truest ending — spoilers

The following route follows the submitted guide and the playable choices:

1. **20 March:** Read the book alone, show Aldred his line and take the manuscript to Abbot Higbald.
2. **24 March:** Obey the abbot and wait for the portent. When the fiery dragons appear, **warn Brother Wilfrith**.
3. **Easter Monday:** The scaffold collapses without anyone on it. Observe that the manuscript **rewrites** the prediction rather than fulfilling the injury.
4. **Rogationtide:** In the Chapter House, **show the rewritten Wilfrith line** and ask Higbald to prepare the house. He accepts the warning and commits to action.
5. **Pentecost:** **Light the beacon on the Heugh** if desired. This is optional for the strongest ending, but changes the striped-sail prophecy.
6. **7 June:** Lead **every soul across the sands before dawn**, taking the Gospel Book and St Cuthbert's coffin.
7. **After the raid:** Choose **“Close the book. Rivet it shut and bury it under the altar again, with the last line unwritten.”**

The resulting ending is **E1 — The Unwritten Page**: the monks and treasures are spared, Wilfrith walks unharmed, and the artifact's unfinished prediction is not allowed to dictate the armarius's future.

## Ethical questions

**Foreknowledge versus responsibility.** Knowing of a coming injury creates a chance to prevent it. Treating a person's suffering as an experiment is a different choice from acting to save them.

**Evidence versus authority.** The armarius must make a cautious abbot believe a dangerous warning, but extraordinary claims can have devastating consequences when no proof is available.

**Political power versus stewardship.** Hunred imagines an all-knowing king rewarding the monastery; the king's ending shows the cost of placing the power to foresee and author events in one ruler's hands.

**Autonomy versus a written fate.** The last line tempts the reader to turn predictive knowledge into an act of control. The strongest ending leaves the sentence—and the future—unfinished.

## Historical background and fictionalization

The original companion guide notes the following historical context:

- The **793 raid on Lindisfarne**, traditionally dated **8 June**, is often used as a landmark for the beginning of the Viking Age in Britain.
- The *Anglo-Saxon Chronicle* describes alarming portents that year, including storms and fiery dragons; suggested natural explanations for the imagery include auroral displays.
- **Higbald** was historically the **bishop** of Lindisfarne, although the playable fiction calls him the abbot. **Alcuin**, a scholar associated with Charlemagne's court, wrote about the attack.
- **Æthelred I of Northumbria** was killed in 796.
- Lindisfarne is a tidal island; the **Pilgrims' Way** is exposed only under suitable tidal conditions.

The armarius's adventure, the iron-bound prophetic book, Brother Wilfrith's scaffold incident and the imagined evacuations are **fictional**. The dates, quotations and historical notes above reflect the **uploaded story guide**; they have **not been independently researched or source-verified** for this README.

## Art direction, interface and accessibility

The visual treatment evokes an illuminated medieval manuscript: **vellum parchment**, iron-gall brown-black ink, **vermilion rubrics**, **lapis blue**, marginal annotations, an iron binding and visible rivets. Narrative passages use an illuminated drop capital.

Typography uses **Uncial Antiqua** for display and **IM Fell English**, with its small-caps variant, for prose and labels. The design supports light/dark themes while keeping the manuscript leaf parchment-colored, a responsive single-column layout below 900px, touch/click and keyboard choices, reduced-motion preferences and three collapsible reference panels on mobile.
