Act I — The Bakery Curse

It is a grey Monday at Kestrel Lane Garage. On the bench sits a paper notebook with a buttery thumbprint on the cover. Its owner, Noor Adeyemi, stands beside it with her arms folded.

Noor: “Twice it's been towed from outside Pell's Bakery. Twice! It starts after a gas stop, it starts after a full day at work. But a Saturday bread run? Click-whirr-whirr, nothing. That street is cursed.”

Her fictional 2013 Wren Aster has already had a new battery (fitted after the first tow) and a new fuel pump (fitted after the second) at another shop. Neither part changed anything. Last Saturday it failed outside the bakery again, and Kestrel Lane collected it on their own flatbed. It stays here until the cause is known.

Ines Marchetti, qualified technician: “You're my reasoning assistant on this one. I do anything with tools; you decide what's worth testing. We have 8 shop hours booked. Noor's notebook costs nothing to read. Every test costs time, and I'll ask for your prediction before I show you a result.”

      Model assumptions and limits
      • All vehicles, places, people, readings and reference ranges are invented for this simulation. None of them are real service specifications.
      • The case labels each piece of information as one of these: symptom (what Noor reports), observation (what the diary or a report shows), controller report (what the engine computer says it sees), independent measurement (what the technician measured directly), inference (what you conclude), and confirmed case finding (an inference backed by independent measurement and a passed retest).
      • An engine controller's reported engine speed is not the same as a direct measurement of the sensor. Many faults can make it read zero.
      • Fourteen trips is a small sample. In real diagnosis, a pattern in a diary is a lead that measurements must corroborate.
